The above is particularly relevant in light of current discussions
on predatory publishing. Indeed, it has been reported that the
lack of quality (or at least the thin quality) of
the papers published in predatory journals is often one defining
trait of this kind of publishing ( Eriksson and Helgesson
2017 : 164-165; see also Xia 2014 ). Previous research
focusing on the (lack of) quality in predatory publishing has
tended to concentrate on the poor rigor of the peer-review
process in this kind of outlets (e.g. Oermann et al.
2018). And while it has also been noted that more
work is needed that examines in more detail the degree
to which articles in predatory journals contain scientific information, as
well as the level of scientific literacy of authors publishing
in them ( Ezinwa Nwagwu and Ojemeni 2015 : 23),
few studies so far have gone down to analyse the
linguistic levels of work published in predatory journals.
