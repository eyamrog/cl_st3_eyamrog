While such studies hold undoubted value for those responsible for
designing care and monitoring and interpreting patient feedback, they also
have limitations in terms of their data and methods of
analysis. Concerning the data used, existing studies have tended to
focus on a relatively small number of responses, usually in
the hundreds ( Lagu et al., 2010 ; López et
al., 2012 ) and at the most in the tens
of thousands ( Raleigh et al., 2009 ), and so
tend to offer fairly limited generalisability with respect to their
findings. Another feature of existing research into patient feedback is
the tendency to examine quantitative responses (e.g. checkbox data) rather
than qualitative responses (e.g. free-text comments). An advantage of checkbox
data is that patients’ responses can be easily quantified to
reveal feedback trends. However, a disadvantage of this type of
data is that patients’ responses are constrained to the questions
and checkbox options in the survey, meaning that patients have
limited (if any) space to provide feedback about the particular
aspects of healthcare that might be important to them, but
which are not covered explicitly by the questions in the
survey.
