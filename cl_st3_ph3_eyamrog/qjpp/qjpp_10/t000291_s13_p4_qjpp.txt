Cda and cl studies such as Al-Hejin's (2015) reveal how
news discourse in particular contributes to the creation of subjectivities
by constructing discursive worlds (e.g., the ‘Middle East’ and the
‘Muslim world’) and subjects to occupy them (e.g., ‘man’ and
‘Muslim woman’), and narratives that link the subjects to the
world and to one another in time. These subjects are
represented as actors (they ‘vote’ and ‘elect’) in events (‘war’,
‘Arab Spring’ and ‘9/11’) that occur in these worlds. By
analysing frequent lexical patterns, scholars can detect how positive and
negative associations discursively form around the subjects who occupy and
perform in these discursive worlds, thereby implicitly creating norms of
behaviour, tastes and preferences for readers who use these categories
in different ways to parse lived experience. To the extent
that the representations of world, event, time and subject in
this news discourse are directed at subjects in the world,
I call the news discourse a ‘first-order’ normative discourse.
