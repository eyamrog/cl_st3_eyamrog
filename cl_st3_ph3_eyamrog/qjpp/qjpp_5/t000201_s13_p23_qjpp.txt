Alterity during the Argaric has also been explored from the
perspective of more local engagements. For example, Aranda Jiménez recently
reassessed the use of typical third millennium BCE burial contexts,
such as megaliths and caves, and showed, through radiocarbon dating
of associated human remains, that their continued use into the
Argaric period was fairly common ( Aranda Jiménez 2013 ).
He argued that instead of seeing intramural burials as the
Argaric norm, and the use of megaliths as atypical or
nonnormative, we should view the Argaric social landscape as heterogeneous
and contentious. He suggested that those who buried their dead
in the older megaliths did so to maintain ancestral ties
to a less hierarchical world and that they materially resisted
the practices of the hierarchical Argaric world. Alterity, from this
perspective, is not inherent to a group's place of origin,
as in traditional colonialist models, but is instead the outcome
of interaction and engagement.
