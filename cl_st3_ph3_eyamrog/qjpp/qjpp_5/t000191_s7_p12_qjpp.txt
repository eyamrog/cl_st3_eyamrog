In thinking along these lines, I take lead from the
foundational essay by Pietz (1985) , “The Problem of the
Fetish.” In arguing that Portuguese understandings of the fetisso (a
word that shared etymological roots from the Latin with “factitious”)
were the products of a highly politicized colonial encounter between
Europeans and West Africans, Pietz made the case that Portuguese
traders were less skeptical of West African social traditions than
they were fearful of and eager to subordinate them. The
simple but long neglected observation we can draw from this
view is that the “ism” of shamanism, along with other
classic precepts in the anthropology of religion ( Asad 1993
), has been born of decidedly fraught encounters.
