Persistent questions in the study of human evolution and paleolithic
archaeology include what counts as modern human behavior, when and
where this behavior began, how it can be detected archaeologically,
and whether behaviors that are archaeologically detectable correspond to fully
developed language capabilities. Cognitive archaeology—or the study of “past ways
of thought as inferred through material remains” ( Renfrew 1994
, p. 3)—has approached these problems by proposing that certain
behaviors evident in the archaeological record, such as complex tool
use, body ornamentation, and figural art, are indicative of the
human mind's ability to think symbolically and therefore as capable
of language.
