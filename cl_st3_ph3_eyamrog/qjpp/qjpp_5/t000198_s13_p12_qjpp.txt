The world's largest representation of a primate, a spider monkey
with a long and curled tail, is a Nazca geoglyph
in Peru (e.g., Reiche 1968 , Cormier & Urbani 2008
) ( Supplemental Figure 1 d ). In the Mediterranean
world, during the half-millennium BCE and early CE, Etruscans and
Romans on the Italian peninsula depicted baboons and tied-up macaques
( Supplemental Table 1 ). Romans also rendered the Moroccan
mosaics of Volubilis that included a vervet ( Supplemental Figure
1 e ), and three centuries later in Constantinople the
Byzantines made a mosaic of a macaque. East of the
Bosphorus, in the Levant, during the caliphate of Al-Walid ibn
Abd al-Málik (ca. AD 674–715) in present-day Jordan, an allochthonous
hamadryas baboon was painted in the context of local and
exotic fauna ( Masseti 2015 ; for an overview about
primates in the Islamic tradition, see Kruk 1995 , Cook
1999 ). An unusual example of the appearance of the
primate motif is a crouched monkey on a medieval stove-tile
recovered in the Baltic city of Tallinn ( Gaimster &
Russow 2011 ). These dissimilar cases support the fact that
circulated primate imageries around the globe were highly valued as
visual and cultural commodities in the past.
