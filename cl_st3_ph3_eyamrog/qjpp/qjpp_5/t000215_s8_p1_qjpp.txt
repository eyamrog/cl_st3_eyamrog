One of the proxies used for the study of past
human behavior is the analysis of the non-masticatory, cultural or
activity-related dental wear, characteristic of the genus Homo since its
appearance ( Koby, 1956 ; Molnar, 1972 ; Brace, 1975
; Puech, 1981 ; Frayer and Russell, 1987 ; Formicola,
1988 ; Lukacs and Pastor, 1988 ). For example, several
specimens of early Homo present toothpick grooves on the interproximal
sides of the teeth, suggesting that grass stalks were intensively
employed as a tool for removing food particles retained between
the teeth and, therefore, it is considered one of the
earliest human cultural habits—in this case, related to oral hygiene
( Frayer and Russell, 1987 ; Formicola, 1988 , 1991
; Turner and Cacciatore, 1998 ; Ungar et al., 2001
; Hlusko, 2003 ; Estalrrich et al., 2020 ).
