Peirce's seeming obsession with the number three has often been
pointed out by his readers. This proliferation of triads is
an effect of his underlying doctrine of firstness, secondness, and
thirdness, 5 which he applies to different aspects of the
semiotic. Firstness, Peirce explains, is “that which is such as
it is positively and regardless of anything else” ( Peirce
1998 , p. 267). Firstness is possibility before any kind
of perception ( Peirce 1998 , pp. 268–69). Secondness is
“that which is as it is in a second something's
being as it is, regardless of any third” ( Peirce
1998 , p. 267). In other words, secondness is the
actual physical experience of the world. “A door is slightly
ajar. You try to open it. Something prevents. You put
your shoulder against it and experience a sense of resistance”
( Peirce 1998 , p. 268). Thirdness is “that which
is as it is as being a Third, or Medium,
between a Second and its First” ( Peirce 1998 ,
p. 160). In other words, a third is that which
brings a first and a second into relation. Beyond the
basic raw physical experience of the world (secondness), thirdness therefore
includes the experience of thought, the formation of habits, the
recognition of patterns, and the formulation of laws. As discussed
below, thirdness is essential for the process of semiosis. In
summary, as explained by Bauer (2002a) , firstness is essence,
secondness is instantiation, and thirdness is relation.
