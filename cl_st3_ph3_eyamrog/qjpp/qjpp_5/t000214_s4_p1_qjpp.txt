We determined the ages at formation of accentuated lines in
the teeth of male and female mandrills and tested the
hypothesis that stress associated with specific life history events affects
tooth formation, and therefore matches the formation of accentuated lines
in tooth sections. Resumption of the mother's sexual swelling cycle
and mate-guarding by males coincided with the formation of accentuated
lines in two animals. One individual formed an accentuated line
that coincided with the birth of one sibling, but not
that of another sibling. In the two female mandrills, all
but one of their sexual swelling cycles and one of
two cases of mate-guarding matched accentuated lines, and in one
case a female's first cycle matched very strong lines in
her teeth. All three parturitions in the two females coincided
with the formation of accentuated lines. We further compared our
histological data with the timing of changes in alpha male
and captures of the mandrills by the CIRMF staff. Changes
in alpha male and captures did not consistently coincide with
accentuated line formation, although repeated captures were associated with lines.
Finally, we advance studies of the relationship between tooth formation
and life histories by using simulations to compare our results
to chance expectations. We found that the number of observed
matches would be very unlikely if accentuated tooth line formation
is unrelated to the timing of life history events. Our
results show that life history events, which are part of
the normal life cycle, can be sufficiently physiologically costly to
affect ameloblast function and leave a record of accentuated line
formation in teeth. These results provide more robust evidence that
accentuated tooth line formation is associated with the timing of
stressful life events than previous studies.
