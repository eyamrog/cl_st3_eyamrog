Intimate intergroup interactions have long been considered the ideal form
of contact for prejudice reduction (Paolini et al., 2021 ).
A construct in its own right, intimacy typically embodies and
encapsulates the elements associated with high-quality contact. Intimate interactions are
voluntarily entered into, pleasant, equal, and cooperative (Islam & Hewstone,
1993 ). Such intimate relationships, more so than casual acquaintances,
are likely to exemplify the conditions identified by Allport (
1954 ) as being integral to prejudice reduction, specifically cooperation,
common goals, equal status, and authority support, as well as
other characteristics thought to augment the contact–prejudice relationship, such as
repeated interactions over time and situation and reciprocal self-disclosure. Although
individuals prone to prejudice are less likely to engage in
intimate intergroup contact (Maunder et al., 2019 ), there is
evidence that they nevertheless benefit from close intergroup associations (see
Turner et al., 2020 , for a review).
