The rich body of archaeological research that has emerged around
settler colonialism in North America has largely taken two distinct
forms: research on Indigenous histories since 1492 and studies of
enslaved Africans and their descendants. The racialized division of settler
colonial studies in archaeology has led to an undertheorization of
the “settler” as a sociopolitical category that transcends ethnic and
racial lines. This disciplinary separation has also hindered a more
complex understanding of the articulations of and frictions between Black
and Indigenous experiences of settler colonialism. Finally, the bifurcation of
archaeological research on Black and Indigenous life under settler colonialism
has left little space for research on non-European “settlers” and
the broad category of “people of color” encapsulated in the
term BIPOC. To date, archaeologists have largely avoided the messy
work of reconstructing the daily lives of people who occupied
multiple racial identities within colonial regimes and have often glossed
over the experiences of communities who do not conventionally fit
within the categories of Black, Indigenous, or European settler.
