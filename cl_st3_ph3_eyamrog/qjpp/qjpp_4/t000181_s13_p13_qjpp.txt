It nearly goes without saying that Risley's attempt to separate
Indian castes into lighter-skinned Aryans, “Negro-like” Dravidians, and East Asian–looking
Mongoloids very closely mirrored the state of European racial thinking
at the time, which also divided humans into three main
groups: Caucasians, Africans, and East Asians—a scheme we see even
today in racist genomics work (e.g., Wade 2015 ). Risley's
work, and the Aryan invasion theory more generally, also clearly
reflects the master narrative of “the opposition of the dark-skinned
savage and the fair-skinned civilized European” ( Trautmann 1997 ,
p. 3). It is striking that this theory has continued
to dominate the scholarship on the peopling of India, even
though it has never been substantiated despite centuries of debate
and research in archaeology, linguistics, and now genomics ( Valiani
2021 ).
