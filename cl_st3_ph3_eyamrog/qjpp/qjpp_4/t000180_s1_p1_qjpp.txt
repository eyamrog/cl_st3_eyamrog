This article reviews key works in the anthropology of mass
incarceration, generated by anthropologists and their interlocutors whose research is
directed outside physical sites of imprisonment. My geographical focus is
on the United States during the last decade's political and
economic Zeitgeist, shaped by the manifestations and consequences of the
carceral state and the prison industrial complex. My discussion is
also guided by research invigorated by anthropology's decolonizing drive and
growing concern about racism within and outside the academy. Along
the way, and emphasized in the final section, I make
the case that anthropology's abiding interest in kinship is a
productive approach for configuring our understanding of the American carceral
state and the racial landscapes of carcerality. The research reviewed
shows how deeply carcerality is embedded in race, illuminating its
destructiveness in Black and brown communities, yet also revealing the
creation of regenerative spaces of kinship.
