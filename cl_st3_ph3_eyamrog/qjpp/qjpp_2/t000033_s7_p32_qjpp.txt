Economic investment shifts have played a critical role in inducing
residential displacement within cities. Specifically, urban geography studies have focused
on how gentrification—the influx of more affluent residents and increases
in commercial and residential property values in urban neighborhoods that
have experienced years of disinvestment and economic decline—forces changes in
racial and income community composition, availability of social and health
services, employment opportunities, and housing availability and affordability ( 81
, 74 , 160 ). These changes are typically driven
by market-oriented urban policies that include the privatization of public
land, changes to homeownership rules, and land rezoning ( 122
). The concept of gentrification has been applied to the
climate adaptation context where policies and strategies result in changes
in the real estate market—driving shifts in capital flow and
development and dislocation of marginalized populations ( 51 , 97
).
