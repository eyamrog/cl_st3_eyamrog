Obtaining reliable estimates of TBI incidence and lifetime prevalence in
the homeless and marginally housed population, as well as in
the general population, has been challenging. Reported incidence of TBI
varies widely across counties, and the methods of sampling participants
and defining TBI cases differ between reports. Considerably higher rates
of TBI have been reported in population-based studies that capture
injuries for which medical attention is not sought, as compared
with studies that gather data from medical records or emergency
departments. Additional sources of bias also exist, including in the
common definitions of TBI, and sampling or referral biases. Similarly,
the method for ascertaining history of TBI has not been
unanimously agreed upon and estimates of the lifetime prevalence of
TBI vary considerably. Meta-analytic work has found that 12% of
the general population reported at least one TBI over the
lifespan. A 2018 state-wide study in Ohio, USA, which used
a modified version of the comprehensive Ohio State University TBI
ascertainment method (a short, structured interview based on the Centers
for Disease Control definition of TBI and endorsed by the
National Institute of Neurological Disorders and Stroke), found an estimated
lifetime prevalence of TBI of 21·7% in the general population,
and a lifetime prevalence of moderate or severe TBI of
2·6%. Although several tools designed to ascertain TBI history are
available (eg, the Ohio State University TBI ascertainment method and
the Brain Injury Screening Questionnaire), these methods have been inconsistently
used in studies of homeless and marginally housed individuals.
