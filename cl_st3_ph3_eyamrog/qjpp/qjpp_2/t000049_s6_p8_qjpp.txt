Identification of factors that affect adult cognitive performance or decline
is important to develop interventions that offer protection against adverse
cognitive outcomes at older ages. Continuing secular increases in access
to education among women and the subsequent impact on cognitive
function could change late-life cognitive outcomes for women in the
future. A commonly cited meta-analysis that found a 50% increased
risk of Alzheimer's disease and related dementias in women compared
with men was primarily based on studies of people born
in the 1920s and earlier, a period during which differences
between sexes in access to education were particularly large. Given
the increase in mean education level over the past century
and the reduction in sex differences in access to education
in some but not all countries, it remains to be
seen whether the increased risk of Alzheimer's disease and related
dementias among women will persist in the future. Our finding
that women in more recent birth cohorts outperform men in
memory and fluency tests and have slower memory decline suggests
that the sex differences in Alzheimer's disease and related dementias
risk might be attenuated in later birth cohorts, due in
part to a secular decrease in sex disparities in access
to education.
