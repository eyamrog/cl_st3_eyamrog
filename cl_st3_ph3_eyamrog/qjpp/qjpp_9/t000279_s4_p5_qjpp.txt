And finally, we have seen an overarching decline of use.
Our hypothesis that this is at least partly due to
the rise of other downtoners will need to be investigated
in future research. Wierzbicka (2006 :28-34) has highlighted the increasing
propensity in British English towards understatement from the eighteenth century
onwards, which she linked to cultural and scientific developments encouraging
both accuracy and moderation. As downtoners are among the linguistic
means for expressing understatement, the complete group of downtoners should
be found to rise in frequency or at least to
stay stable. Should this not be the case, Wierzbicka’s (2006)
proposal, which she corroborates with the number of downtoner types
available in English (not their token frequency), might need to
be re-examined. The overarching cultural trend may be less clear
than she assumes or it may manifest itself in different
registers and genres to a variable extent. With its competing
demands on speakers to appear truthful, clear, and credible, the
courtroom context may have inhibited the use of items that,
after all, introduce fuzziness.
