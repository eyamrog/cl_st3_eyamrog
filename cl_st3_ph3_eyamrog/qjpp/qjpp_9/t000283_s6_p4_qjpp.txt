The only genre effect found in this study was a
more pronounced centralization and near-merger of /ɪr/ and /ɛr/ in
non-broadcast talks. The tendency for a more closely spaced array
of /ɪr/, /ɛr/, and /ʌr/ to emerge in the arguably
less formal genre non-broadcast talks harmonizes with Wells’ (1982:407) association
between the distinctness (of vowel categories) and more prestigious accents,
particularly in the west of Scotland. We would argue that
our findings reflect this tendency at a purely stylistic level,
but the pattern will need to be investigated more rigorously
across a broader range of genres.
