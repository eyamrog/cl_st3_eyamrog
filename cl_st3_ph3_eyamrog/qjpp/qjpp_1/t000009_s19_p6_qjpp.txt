All psychiatric disorders were significantly associated with an increased risk
of subsequent self-harm (Fig. 3c and Supplementary Table 6 ).
Self-harm risk was observed to change according to the length
of follow-up, with significantly higher HRs observed within 12 months
of psychiatric disorder diagnosis. Individuals with depression were estimated to
be 44 times more likely to self-harm during the first
year (adjusted HR, 44.1; CI, 34.0–57.1). Patients with anxiety disorder
or schizophrenia were 21 times (HR, 21.1; CI, 16.4–27.0) and
7 times (HR, 7.5; CI, 5.0–11.2) more likely to self-harm,
respectively, during the first year. Patients with substance use disorder
were 4 times (HR, 4.5; CI, 3.7–5.3) more likely to
self-harm within the first year (Fig. 3c and Supplementary Table
6 ). The risk of self-harm markedly decreased over subsequent
years of follow-up among individuals with depression (HR, 5.2; CI,
4.6–5.8), anxiety disorder (HR, 4.0; CI, 3.5–4.5), schizophrenia (HR, 2.9;
CI, 2.4–3.5), bipolar disorder (HR, 12.1; CI, 7.5–19.5), personality disorder
(HR, 9.0; CI, 2.9–28.5) and substance abuse (HR, 1.9; CI,
1.7–2.0) (Fig. 3c and Supplementary Table 6 ).
