Comparisons between rural and urban areas often presume that all
rural places and spaces are the same and create “geographic
imaginaries,” which stereotype people, environment, culture, and practices by others
who do not occupy rural spaces ( 64 ). This
dichotomization of the rural–urban divide has dominated conceptualizations of urban
and rural spaces. Yet, the continuity of rural–urban spaces is
likely a more relevant way to understand the complexity of
rural life and the impact of geography, culture, and tradition
on health. As Meserole ( 82 ) noted in his
discussion of rural, “It is not possible to justly indicate
the place at which rurality ends—or where urban conditions begin”
(p. 233).
