Ethical considerations are often slower to emerge beneath the veneer
of optimism and determinism surrounding technologies in health ( 63
). Gómez-Ramírez and colleagues ( 19 ) argue in favor
of actions, not just considerations, that promote ethical, equity, and
social justice implications for mHealth. Kellerman ( 27 ) penned
a brief for The Atlantic on the 2013 mHealth Summit
in Washington, DC. Remembering this was a time of boisterous
enthusiasm for mHealth, she wrote, “With any luck, these apps,
and the host of other personalized digital technologies out there,
will let consumers handle as many health problems as they
can, safely, on their own, freeing overburdened physicians to devote
their time to matters that really do require FDA regulation”
( 27 ). In the context of private-sector investment in
mHealth, with both established ventures in the digital health space,
such as Babylon and 1mg, alongside new start-ups, concerns about
accountability, quality, and safety are reasonable. The most obvious and
well-described risks relate to the safety and confidentiality of sharing
data or personal health information via mobile devices ( 19
).
