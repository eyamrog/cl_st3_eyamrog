In the first systematic review conducted for this article, we
identified 21 papers that attempt to quantify the health cobenefits
of climate policies. Of these, nine were conducted in Europe
(mostly Western Europe), five in North America, and seven in
the rest of the world, including the Global North and
Global South. The results are presented in Supplemental Table 1
, and the criteria for our selection of papers from
an initial set of 8,878 articles are set out in
the Supplemental Material . Transport, land use, and buildings (residential
and commercial, including energy efficiency and heating practices, for example)
were the three most studied sectors; the authors agreed that
these have the most potential for realizing multiple health cobenefits
(see Supplemental Figure 1 ). Land-use management and sustainable transportation
in particular are inherently interconnected, with wide agreement in the
published literature that a redesign of modern cities involving a
shift away from private vehicles would yield cobenefits in terms
of improved air quality, increased physical activity, social cohesion, and
access to green spaces ( 61 ).
