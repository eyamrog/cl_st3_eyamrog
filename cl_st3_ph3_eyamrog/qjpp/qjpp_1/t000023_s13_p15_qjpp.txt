The quantification of how different types of factors (or sets
of factors) contribute to or mediate social differences (e.g., percentage
of effects mediated through different factors) has been challenging, owing
to the rudimentary nature of many of the mediation analyses
used, often simple comparisons of associations before and after adjustment,
many times using cross-sectional data. Even when longitudinal data are
available and when more sophisticated approaches are used, the quantification
of mediation is rendered very complex because of the presence
of confounders, the multiple mediators likely involved, and the possible
interactions between mediators and between mediators and social antecedents (
39 ). Perhaps the main contribution of mediation studies has
been the demonstration of how pervasive the social patterning is
of many behavioral, psychosocial, and biological factors linked to health.
The fact that all these factors are consistently and strongly
socially patterned has buttressed the argument that the social world
is a major determinant of health. This patterning demonstrates the
notion of embodiment, i.e., the process by which the social
world has specific and measurable impacts on biological structure and
function ( 69 ).
